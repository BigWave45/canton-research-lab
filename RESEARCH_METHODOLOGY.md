# Research Methodology

## Purpose

Develop original, useful research for consideration by the Canton Community Research Grants programme. The research orientation is interdisciplinary, with quantitative analysis used where appropriate. The expected output is a substantive paper supported by reproducible models, documented data, calculations, and visualisations.

This methodology studies prior funded projects to understand the characteristics of fundable research. It does not claim to reverse-engineer confidential selection decisions or predict grant outcomes.

## 1. Comparative grant-winner intelligence

### Programme sample

Build a documented sample across:
- Canton Foundation research and ecosystem funding programmes;
- Ethereum Foundation;
- Stellar Development Foundation;
- Uniswap Foundation;
- Solana Foundation and other relevant protocol research programmes;
- selected traditional financial-market research institutions where their work is relevant.

Target at least 30 projects if public records permit. If fewer are available or comparable, report the actual sample and explain the limitation. Do not pad the sample with weak matches.

### Unit of analysis

A funded project, not merely an organisation or a grant announcement.

For each project capture:
- programme and funding track;
- award year and funding period;
- recipient and publicly stated affiliation;
- project title and research question;
- discipline(s);
- problem and intended beneficiaries;
- prior literature or context cited by the project;
- method and data;
- original contribution;
- deliverables promised;
- deliverables actually published or released;
- evidence of subsequent use, citation, adoption, or influence;
- amount and duration, only when disclosed;
- source URLs, access dates, and confidence in each field.

### Comparability controls

Do not combine academic research grants, developer grants, public-goods funding, hackathon prizes, and discretionary content grants as if they were one selection process.

Classify each programme by:
- primary mandate;
- eligible recipient type;
- expected output;
- selection mechanism, if public;
- award structure;
- evaluation criteria, if public.

Analyse unlike programme types separately, then identify cautiously stated cross-programme patterns.

## 2. Completed-output examination

For each selected project, seek the actual paper, dataset, code, model, report, or deployed tool.

Record:
- what was ultimately delivered;
- whether the output answers the stated question;
- whether the method is inspectable;
- whether data or code is available;
- what changed in the field or ecosystem, if evidence exists.

A grant announcement alone is insufficient evidence of research quality or impact.

## 3. Comparative analysis

Code projects against these observable dimensions:

1. Problem specificity.
2. Importance to a defined audience.
3. Originality relative to existing work.
4. Methodological transparency.
5. Empirical or theoretical rigour.
6. Interdisciplinary integration.
7. Reproducibility.
8. Quality and accessibility of the deliverable.
9. Demonstrable downstream utility.
10. Fit with the programme's publicly stated mandate.

Use a consistent rubric with anchored definitions. Preserve the evidence supporting every coded judgement. Where a dimension cannot be assessed, mark it unknown rather than assigning an arbitrary score.

The analysis may identify recurring characteristics. It must not claim that a characteristic caused funding unless the funder has explicitly documented that reason.

## 4. Canton research landscape

Map the public research and technical record across:
- financial economics and market structure;
- institutional finance and post-trade workflows;
- collateral, liquidity, and settlement;
- privacy, confidentiality, and verification;
- distributed systems and synchronisation;
- interoperability and composability;
- governance, incentives, and network economics;
- compliance and regulatory constraints.

For each area, record established knowledge, active debate, existing Canton-specific work, available evidence, and unresolved questions.

## 5. Hypothesis generation

Develop at least five research hypotheses. Each must specify:
- precise research question;
- why the question matters;
- existing literature and closest prior work;
- what is not yet answered;
- proposed contribution;
- relevant disciplines and how they interact;
- required data and access conditions;
- analytical method;
- feasibility and resource requirements;
- intended outputs;
- limitations and risks.

Do not select a topic based on novelty claims alone. Verify the closest prior work first.

## 6. Quantitative and mathematical work

Choose methods based on the research question and data, not on a desire to appear technical. Potential methods include statistical inference, network analysis, optimisation, mechanism design, game theory, stochastic modelling, time-series analysis, simulation, and scenario analysis.

Every quantitative result must document:
- variable definitions and units;
- data source, collection date, and coverage;
- inclusion and exclusion rules;
- transformations and missing-data treatment;
- assumptions;
- equations or algorithm;
- implementation or calculation steps;
- uncertainty and sensitivity;
- limitations and reproducibility instructions.

Clearly distinguish observed measurements, estimates, model outputs, and illustrative scenarios. Do not present simulated results as observed network behaviour.

## 7. Interdisciplinary integration

The disciplines must jointly answer the research question rather than appear as disconnected sections.

Example: a settlement-efficiency question may require distributed-systems analysis to define execution and finality, institutional-finance analysis to define the workflow, financial economics to identify relevant costs and risks, and quantitative modelling to estimate differences under explicit assumptions.

Explain how each discipline changes the model, interpretation, or conclusions.

## 8. Research outputs

Expected package:
- research paper;
- source and literature review;
- dataset or documented data extract, subject to rights and availability;
- reproducible calculations or code where feasible;
- model and assumptions documentation;
- figures and visualisations;
- concise research brief.

Do not promise a dataset, codebase, or empirical result before feasibility is established.

## 9. Research management and decision records

Maintain:
- programme register;
- funded-project register;
- completed-output register;
- source ledger;
- Canton landscape map;
- hypothesis register;
- data dictionary;
- assumptions register;
- analysis notebooks/scripts;
- decision log;
- project state checkpoint.

Every significant decision should record its rationale and supporting evidence.

## 10. Initial execution sequence

1. Verify the Canton grant announcement, formal terms, eligibility, deadlines, and submission process.
2. Build the programme register.
3. Assemble the funded-project sample, aiming for 30 well-documented projects where feasible.
4. Inspect completed outputs.
5. Code comparable projects using the rubric.
6. Summarise patterns separately by programme type.
7. Map Canton's research landscape and existing literature.
8. Identify evidence gaps and data availability.
9. Develop five interdisciplinary hypotheses with quantitative feasibility assessments.
10. Present the comparative findings and hypotheses for a topic decision.

No article drafting or final topic selection before these steps are complete.
